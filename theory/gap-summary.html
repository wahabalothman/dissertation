How Conspiratorial Claims Acquire Political Authority: A Gap in the Literature

PLACEHOLDER. Drafted with AI assistance and not yet written or verified by me. To be rewritten.

The question and the model

This dissertation asks under what conditions political elites adopt conspiratorial claims circulating outside established institutions, and whether the origin attributed to such a claim affects its political acceptability independently of its content and its endorser. I approach it through a four-stage sequence: fringe origin (imageboards such as 4chan/pol), spread through alt-media (Rumble, long-form podcasts, Telegram), elite uptake, and non-uniform electorate response. The sequence is a hypothesis, not an organizing assumption. The literature reviewed below already documents claims that begin with elites, claims assembled jointly by elites and audiences, and claims that never reach elites, and a design that cannot represent those cases cannot test the pathway. The gap this review identifies is bounded. It is not that transmission, elite amplification, or heterogeneous response are unstudied; each is. It is that, within the population of claims circulating in the U.S. alt-media ecosystem, no located study explains which claims elites select against a comparison set of claims they did not, or tests whether attributed origin moves political acceptance separately from belief.

Origin: “legitimacy” is five constructs

Barkun (2003; 2016) supplies the anchor: conspiracy theories as “stigmatized knowledge,” rejected by truth-validating institutions and “mainstreamed” when fringe and center merge. He offers a category, not a measure, and no located study follows the same claims over time and dates that crossing. Adjacent work measures adjacent things. Buehling, Zhang, and Heft (2026), comparing New World Order and Great Replacement discourse across 4chan, Twitter, and alternative news media over 2011–2021, find both platform-specific styles and convergence in how conspiracism is expressed, without identifying what drives a platform’s boundary to move. Ekman (2022) shows replacement claims being rebuilt for mainstream-right audiences, so what crosses is an argument structure, not a verbatim proposition. Pyrhönen, Bauvois, Berg, and Holley (2026), in 1,612 life-course interviews, find unprompted conspiracy talk in a tenth of transcripts and that its pandemic-era rise came substantially from passages challenging conspiracy theories. Visibility is not acceptance.

Two literatures outside conspiracy research bear directly on what “acceptance” means. Bursztyn, Egorov, and Fiorin (2020) show that shifting perceptions of public support changed willingness to express xenophobic views and the sanctions attached to doing so. Muirhead and Rosenblum (2019) describe a “new conspiracism” that works by repetition and social validation (“a lot of people are saying”) rather than by evidence, with consequences for political standing and institutions rather than for belief. Both are close precursors to the argument here, and both are theoretical or adjacent in domain. The origin stage therefore has to keep separate at least five constructs the conspiracy literature conflates as “legitimacy”: institutional endorsement, elite political endorsement, reduced social stigma, public visibility, and perceived credibility. The dissertation’s contribution beyond Bursztyn et al. and Muirhead and Rosenblum is to specify what conspiratorial content, intermediary transformation, and attributed provenance add to their mechanisms, and to test propositions that both leave untested.

Spread: what intermediaries do to a claim

The second stage asks what happens to a claim between its first appearance and its arrival in elite speech. The computational literature has answered the descriptive half of that question. Zannettou et al. (2018) estimated cross-platform influence among 4chan/pol, Reddit, Twitter, and Gab with Hawkes processes on hashed image memes, and coverage has since reached the platforms this model names: Telegram forwarding (Hoseini et al. 2024), Rumble podcast transcripts (Balci et al. 2025), and political podcasts, where election-fraud claims appeared in more than a quarter of episodes from 79 major shows between the 2020 election and January 6 (Wirtschafter 2023). The unit of analysis has moved from memes and links to text: Hanley and Durumeric (2024a) apply Hawkes modeling to paragraph-level embeddings across 732 Telegram channels, and Gerard, Luceri, Blas, and Ferrara (2026) build platform-invariant discourse networks from 5.7 million posts across X, TikTok, Truth Social, and Telegram during the 2024 election, predict cross-platform narrative emergence days ahead with Springfield as a demonstration, and have announced an extension to podcasts (USC Viterbi, 24 June 2026). Heft and Buehling (2022) have already set out the choice between reference-based and content-based diffusion measurement and the risks of selecting predefined actors and topics. That a claim moves across platforms, and roughly how fast, is established.

What is not established is what the claim is when it arrives. Every study above measures whether a narrative reappears, not how it changed on the way. Ekman (2022) shows in the Great Replacement case that intermediaries rebuild the argument for a new audience, but as a single-case reading, not as a coded comparison across many claims. And no located study includes elite speech as a node, so none can say whether the version an elite adopts is the fringe version, the podcast version, or something assembled at the point of adoption. Gerard et al.’s own result cuts the same way: adoption is predictable from network proximity even without direct cross-platform influence, which means reappearance can be forecast while transmission remains unidentified, and first observed appearance is not authorship.

That is the job of stage two in this dissertation. The corpus is coded at the level of the proposition (alleged conspirators, covert action, purpose, evidence offered, speaker stance, and any attributed source), and each claim’s successive versions are compared. The question is whether intermediaries supply evidence, strip stigmatized language, alter the alleged conspiracy, or attribute the claim to a source other than where it was first observed. The last of these is where stage two feeds the rest of the model: if podcasts and alt-media are where “people are saying” gets attached to claims, then asserted provenance is manufactured at this stage, elite selection at stage three can be tested against which transformed version was available, and the experiment at stage four tests a cue that the corpus shows is actually in circulation.

Elite uptake: the literature already argues against a one-way pipeline

Here prior work competes rather than leaves a gap. Starbird, DiResta, and DeButts (2023) reconstruct three 2020 voter-fraud rumors and find “participatory disinformation”: elites set a frame, ordinary users supply apparent evidence, intermediaries select and reframe it, elites amplify the result. Benkler et al. (2020), on 55,000 stories, five million tweets, and 75,000 Facebook posts, conclude the mail-in fraud campaign was led by elites and mass media with social media in support. Bergmann (2025) argues strategic weaponization from above. Uscinski et al. (2022) find no systematic increase in aggregate conspiracy belief across their measures, which does not show supply is inconsequential but does mean any supply-side claim must be about which claims, which believers, and which elites, not about more belief overall. On the elite side, Blackington and Cayton (2025) code a decade of Polish Law and Justice speech into dog-whistles and open “barks,” and Blackington and Cayton (2024) find, against the elite-cue literature, that ruling-party tweets endorsing conspiracy theories earned less engagement than non-official ones and that threat frames, not elite identity, drove longevity. Elite endorsement does not uniformly confer an advantage, which is relevant to selection even though explaining engagement is not explaining an elite’s first adoption.

What none of this answers is selection. Why do some circulating claims receive elite endorsement while comparable claims stall? Showing that an ordinary user posted first establishes neither that the elite felt compelled to adopt it (audience capture), nor that the elite chose it to fit a prior frame (strategic selection), nor that the elite had solicited such material (joint production). Answering that requires a sample of claims identified independently of whether they became prominent, including claims first observed with grassroots users, with intermediaries, and with elites, and including claims that failed to cross a stage. A trace that starts from elite speech and works backward selects on the outcome. Springfield, documented step by step by NewsGuard, NBC, and ABC, still exists only as journalism and as a demonstration case, and any analysis of it needs stalled comparison cases beside it.

Response: factual backfire is unreliable; provenance and acceptance are untested

Factual backfire is not a reliable general expectation. Wood and Porter (2019) found none across 52 issues and 10,100 subjects; Prike, Blackley, Swire-Thompson, and Ecker (2023) found none on rating-scale measures in three experiments, with a measure-dependent exception on an open-ended response designed to elicit it; Nyhan (2021) concedes it is rare. The model should rest on non-uniform belief movement, for which there is direct evidence: Berlinski et al. (2023) show fraud allegations reduced election confidence particularly among Republicans; Lyons and Workman (2022) show explicit fraud cues raise co-partisan belief while depressing confidence broadly, and implicit cues do nothing; Radnitz (2022) finds official conspiracy claims had little effect in Georgia and Kazakhstan once motivated reasoning was accounted for. Fahey (2023) matters for how the belief measure is read: accuracy-oriented treatments failed to reduce endorsement of 2020-election and COVID misperceptions among Republicans and in one condition increased it, so reported belief is substantially sincere. Any argument that acceptance moves separately from belief has to be compatible with that.

On origin and acceptance, the nearest studies are close but not on target. Bram (2021) shows experimentally that official legitimization of a formerly fringe claim (the 2021 UFO report) warmed attitudes toward conspiracy theorists; this is the closest precursor to a normalization argument, and the expectation that attributed origin shifts acceptance is derived from it, though attitudes toward believers are not permission for politicians to advance a claim. Connor Desai and Reimers (2023) vary whether misinformation is explained as deliberate or accidental and find no difference, which concerns the falsehood’s cause, not its authorship. No located experiment holds claim and endorser constant while randomizing attributed origin, and none measures political acceptance separately from belief. Those are distinct constructs, and acceptance itself needs decomposing: permission to investigate, permission to assert publicly, willingness to excuse an unsupported assertion, and support for acting on it are different, and a respondent endorsing free inquiry is not endorsing the allegation.

Three meanings of origin

The review to this point, and the four-stage model itself, slide between three things. Documented provenance is where a claim’s development can actually be traced. Asserted provenance is where elites and intermediaries say it came from. Perceived provenance is where an audience thinks it came from. These diverge: an elite-originated claim can be presented as something ordinary people discovered, and audiences can infer grassroots origin with no explicit cue. Muirhead and Rosenblum’s title is asserted provenance as rhetoric. This distinction connects the chapters. The observational chapter codes how speakers attribute claims (to citizens, experts, anonymous sources, other elites) as part of the text already being coded, which also establishes whether provenance cues occur often enough outside an experiment to matter. The experiment tests whether those attributions move acceptance while holding the allegation, stated evidence, and apparent breadth of public support constant.

Two problems any answer must survive

First, identification. Shojaie and Fox (2022) lay out why predictive time-series relationships are not causal; regime changes masquerade as Hawkes self-excitation (Filimonov and Sornette 2015); unobserved subprocesses create spurious cross-platform edges (Jin and Huang 2026). A finding that 4chan “led” a podcast is, without a control for the offline event that moved both, consistent with both reacting to the same cable segment, which is why causal weight cannot rest on transmission modeling. Second, the conspiratorial specificity question. A committee member will ask whether the same result would follow for any unverified accusation or partisan message. The experiment therefore needs a non-conspiratorial accusation arm, and the theory needs to say why allegations of covert coordination should behave differently from ordinary accusations of incompetence. A third, forward-looking problem: Hanley and Durumeric (2024b) estimate machine-generated articles grew sharply on small misinformation sites after ChatGPT’s release, and Costello et al. (2026, preprint) show language-model dialogues can increase as well as decrease conspiracy belief, so a human fringe incubator is an assumption to defend.

Mechanism, rivals, and what would force revision

The central mechanism: attributing a claim to grassroots origin lowers the reputational cost of advancing it, for elites and for the citizens who tolerate them, by signaling that the claim is already socially validated and that endorsing it is responsiveness rather than invention. This predicts that grassroots attribution raises political acceptance more than it raises belief, that the effect is larger among co-partisans and among respondents high in perceived social sanction, and that elites attribute claims to citizens more often when the claim is stigmatized.

Two serious rivals. Persuasion: attribution moves acceptance only through belief, because “many people say it” is treated as evidence; if so, acceptance and belief move together and the difference between them is not distinguishable from zero. Partisan usefulness: acceptance tracks what the claim does for the respondent’s side regardless of attribution; if so, the origin manipulation does nothing once partisan alignment is controlled, and the observational chapter finds elites attribute claims to citizens no more often when stigmatized than when not.

Three findings that would force revision. If the C-versus-D contrast (grassroots versus elite origin, endorsement held constant) is null on acceptance with adequate power, the provenance mechanism fails and the dissertation becomes a study of selection alone. If acceptance and belief move together and a direct test of the difference is null, the normalization framing gives way to persuasion. If the coded corpus shows elites rarely assert provenance at all, the cue is not part of political rhetoric and the experiment measures a counterfactual, not a practice. The pilot exists to see the third before the other two are built.
